I. Background: what we wanted to know

This report analyzes a survey sent out in 2026 by Jothon, a working group within g0v.tw1, Taiwan's grassroots civic tech community. With personal details removed, the raw responses are open to everyone under CC BY 4.0, credited to the survey respondents ("臺灣公民科技推廣問卷填寫者").

The survey gathered 127 responses up to 20 July 2026. We wanted to see what Taiwan's civic tech community really looks like: who takes part, and what people at different levels of involvement need and struggle with. By finding out what draws people in and where they get stuck, we hope to help g0v.tw Jothon put its resources where they matter most and keep Taiwan's civic tech community thriving.

Put simply, civic tech means using technology and digital tools to make public life better. It can take many forms: building a small tool that makes it easier to take part in public affairs or use public services, or bringing people together to discuss policy and using open data to improve how government works. Open data here means data that governments or organizations publish for anyone to freely use. Examples include the "mask map", which helped people in Taiwan find face masks during COVID-19, and vTaiwan, where people with different views discuss policy online.

If you want to go straight to projects you could join, Civic Tech Taiwan, a database run by g0v.tw Jothon, lists many projects and guides to getting involved (in Chinese). It is a good place to start.

II. Summary: four key findings

  1. 1. Stalled projects and burned-out core members are the biggest pain for Builders

    At every g0v.tw bimonthly hackathon, you see plenty of projects looking for people to "jump in" (community slang for joining a project) and build with them. But for people who work on projects, the biggest headache is not a shortage of people. It is keeping progress steady, getting results to make a difference, and core members burning out. In this survey, 32% (15/47) of the 47 Builders had no active project when they answered. Progress that stops and starts, results that don't land, and a few people holding on for a long time: together, these three are the biggest obstacle. Among these respondents, those with more say in a project tend to be more likely to burn out. This is quite likely because more say usually means more effort and time, which wears people out more.

  2. 2. Believing in what a project wants to do, and making like-minded friends, make people more likely to stay

    The strongest pull, both for bringing people into civic tech projects and for keeping them, is believing in what the project aims to achieve. It is the most common reason people join, and the most lasting reason they stay. Another change worth noting: the motive "build up a portfolio and sharpen my skills" grew from 6 people at first to 9 now, a relative increase of 50%. This shows that some people keep taking part to build their skills.

  3. 3. Not knowing what projects exist, and having no one to show the way, are the biggest barriers for newcomers

    Among the 26 Curious who have not taken part, the most common barriers were "don't know which projects or issues exist" and "no one around to show me the way, and I worry I won't fit in or be any help", followed by "don't know how to join or where to start". "I feel I'd need to know how to code; the bar is too high", which many people assume is the main obstacle, actually came only fourth. Of all respondents, 65% regularly see news from at least one official g0v.tw channel, but seeing the news does not mean knowing what you can do, or knowing someone you can ask.

  4. 4. A clearly explained problem and a working prototype attract support most easily

    Among the 27 respondents who have given financial support to civic tech projects, what mattered most when deciding whether to support one was "the problem it tackles is important and clearly explained", and whether it "matches issues I care about". More than half of these funders also felt that projects "still at a very early stage, just an idea" have the hardest time getting resources. This makes sense: projects that have not yet turned an idea into a working prototype usually find it harder to win funders over.

III. Findings

1. People from many walks of life, over 60% living in Taipei or New Taipei

This chapter looks at the backgrounds of the people who answered the survey and where they usually spend their time.

1.1 Who they are and where they are

👥
Key point

Of the 127 respondents, only 47 have actually worked on a project. Most have not.

Read more

The 127 responses fall roughly into three groups:

  • The Curious: 26 people who have heard of or seen civic tech, but have not actually got involved.
  • The Users: 54 people who know the community but have not worked on any project.
  • The Builders: 47 people who have actually worked on at least one civic tech project. Later questions about "what got stuck" and "what keeps people going" were answered only by these 47, because they asked about specific project experience.

Most respondents are aged 31 to 50: 35% are 31 to 40 and 29% are 41 to 50, while only 5.5% are 20 or under. By gender, 50% are men, 41% women, and 9% other or prefer not to say.

Respondents come from a wide range of work backgrounds: freelancers and nonprofit workers are the largest groups, at 20% each, followed by schools or research institutions (16%), companies or startups (16%), students (13%) and the public sector (8%). Of the 55 who told us more about their organization, 27% work at nonprofits and associations, 25% at academic and research institutions, and 22% at companies and startups. People who care about civic tech come from many industries and organizations, not one field.

But looking only at where people live understates how far their connections reach. The survey also asked "Besides where you live, which regions do you have close ties to?" 63 people answered: 34 named places in central, southern or eastern Taiwan (such as Taichung, Tainan, Kaohsiung and Hualien), and 14 mentioned places overseas. Even among the 79 who live in Taipei or New Taipei, 20 also have close ties to central, southern or eastern Taiwan, and 8 to places overseas. Most respondents live in the capital region, but their personal networks reach much further.

1.2 Have all the people who care about civic tech been to g0v.tw?

📡
Key point

Of all respondents, 47% have never been to a g0v.tw event. Getting the news does not mean knowing what you can do, or having someone to ask.

Read more

Of all respondents, 47% (60/127) have never been to a g0v.tw event. Even among the 47 Builders, 28% (13/47) have never been to a g0v.tw event.

When asked "Where do you get information about civic tech?", 59% chose "nonprofit organizations", more than the 54% who chose "open source communities (including g0v.tw)". g0v.tw is an important hub in the ecosystem, but not the only way in.

2. Most are lightly involved, mainly as users

2.1 What roles have people played in civic tech projects?

🎭
Key point

Of the 101 who have had contact with civic tech, nearly nine in ten have been a "User". Only 33% have actually helped build or maintain a project.

Read more

The survey asked which roles people have played in civic tech projects (more than one answer allowed). Among the 101 who have had contact with civic tech: 89% have used a tool or joined a discussion, 48% have helped put something into use or spread it, 33% have helped plan, build, design or maintain a project, 28% have started a project, and 17% have provided money or mobilized resources.

46 people (46%) ticked only one role; just 10 (10%) ticked all five. A large group sticks to one thing, while a small group does almost everything, and the spread in between is uneven. The more deeply involved people are, the more roles they usually take on at once.

The survey also asked a single-choice question: "Which statement best describes what you do in the civic tech community?" The five options run from the least to the most say over a project's direction: Follow (read about issues and results), Report (provide information when a project asks, such as road conditions or pollution data), Join (help get things done on projects others started), Co-design (define the problem and design the solution with others) and Start (spot a problem, find partners and launch a project). Another 6 people chose "None of these fits me", which is left off this scale. Of the 101, 41% chose the lightest level, "Follow".

Of the 46 who ticked only one role, 42 ticked the one with the lowest bar, "used a tool or joined a discussion", and 30 of them were at the "Follow" level of involvement. "Doing only one thing" largely reflects "being lightly involved".

One more thing worth noting: people who build projects have not necessarily used other people's civic tech. 38 people ticked "helped plan, build, design or maintain" or "started a project" (counting those who ticked both only once), and 7 of them (18%) did not tick "used a tool or joined a discussion".

2.2 What are these projects about?

🧩
Key point

Among the 47 Builders, the most common project topic was environment and ecology (15%). The rest are spread across 10 areas such as education, disaster response and political oversight.

Read more

Each of the 47 described in one sentence "what problem their most recent project aims to solve". After sorting these by hand into 11 categories, the most common topic was environment and ecology, with 7 people (15%). Second was "Other", with 6 people (13%), covering topics such as housing, health care, energy and digital identity. Next came education and learning, community-building and collaboration tools, and disaster prevention and public safety, with 5 people each (11%); then political oversight and open parliament, land use, cities and local communities, and public discussion and opinion tools, with 4 each (9%). The rest were transport and roads (3 people), working with government and civil servants (2), and fact-checking misinformation (2).

Of these, 15 people's projects deal with topics that require working with the public sector. This fits with Chapter 7, where "Working with government" was the most wanted event.

The survey looks at the same people from two angles: their overall experience, not tied to any one project (this chapter), and "the project they worked on most recently" (Chapters 3 to 5). The two are kept separate, because someone who often starts projects in the community may not be the founder of the project they worked on most recently.

3. What wears Builders down most: stalled progress and burned-out core members

3.1 What troubles people most when working on a project?

😣
Key point

Among the 47 Builders, the top three problems were keeping progress steady, getting results to make a difference, and a few people holding everything up. "Not finding partners", the one most people would think of first, ranked only fourth.

Read more

Only the 47 Builders answered these questions. The survey listed 13 common difficulties and asked them to rate each one. We use two measures: a rating of 3 or higher (3, 4 and 5 combined) means the problem really held back progress; 4 or higher (4 and 5 combined) means it was bad enough to make them want to quit.

The top three problems were keeping progress steady, getting results to make a difference, and a few people holding everything up. "Can't find partners who want to work on it together", the one most people would think of first, ranked fourth. These three may feed into each other: when momentum breaks, results are slow to appear; without results, it is harder to show the project is worth the effort; fewer people step in, and the load falls back on the few.

⚖️

Among the Builders there is a pattern: the more say someone has in a project, the more likely they are to feel burned out. Of the 10 who started their own project, 6 (60%) said core-member burnout clearly held back progress.

3.3 Decision-makers don't see what newcomers struggle with

👁️
Key point

When planning how to bring newcomers in, asking only founders and decision-makers may not get you the answer: it is usually not a problem they face themselves.

Read more

On the item "Newcomers or people who can't code don't know how they can help", the people who feel it most are at the shallow end: 2 of 6 (33%) in the Report group, 1 of 5 (20%) in the Follow group, 1 of 8 (12%) in the Join group, 0% (0/18) in the Co-design group, and 1 of 10 (10%) in the Start group.

People less involved were more likely to worry about how newcomers who can't code could help. As people gain more say in a project, this worry broadly gets lower. So if you want to plan how to bring newcomers in, asking only founders and decision-makers may not get you the answer, because it is not a problem they face themselves.

🛠️
Key point

The two most common responses pointed in opposite directions: reaching out for help, or slowing down and lowering expectations. Of the 28 who answered, 11 chose to lower their expectations or took no particular action.

The survey also asked an optional question, "Did you take any action to deal with these difficulties?" 28 people answered.

The two most common responses pointed in opposite directions: reaching out for help (6 people), or slowing down and lowering expectations (5 people). "Retention rate" means the same person chose the same motive both times. "Net change" looks at how the number of people rose or fell: those who chose a motive when they first joined, plus those who picked it up later, minus those who no longer give it as a reason, shown as a percentage of the starting number. The two look at different things, so they rank motives differently. For example, "People around me were doing it, so I joined" had the lowest retention rate of the six, at 67%; but because the 4 people who dropped it matched the 4 who picked it up, its net change is still 0% and the total did not change.

Believing in a project's goal is the most common reason people take part, and the reason that best sustains them over time.

Identifying with the community had the biggest net drop of the six motives: 20 people chose it at first, and 17 do now. Of the original 20, 6 (30%) no longer give it as a reason, while 3 others picked it up later. This is worth looking into: did the people who stopped naming the community as a reason gradually lose their sense of belonging along the way?

Only two motives grew overall: "building a portfolio and sharpening skills" and "Meet new people and like-minded friends". The first grew more, and it is a goal-oriented motive.

Looking only at why people stay, there are some differences by age. Among those 30 and under, 4 of 10 (40%) chose "building a portfolio and sharpening skills", higher than 19% across all Builders. Among those 41 and over, "believing in the goal" stands out at 89%, and the biggest gaps are "just for fun" and "identifying with the community", 21 and 20 percentage points below the overall figures.

But each age group has only 10 to 19 people, so these generational differences are an early observation, worth tracking again as more responses come in.

5. Personal networks are the resource projects use most

5.1 Which resources do projects use most?

🧰
Key point

Among the 47 Builders, personal networks were the most used resource (66%), ahead of funding (47%).

Read more

Which resources did the 47 Builders use on their most recent project? Personal networks came first, used by 66%, followed by "Funding" (47%), then public or open data (45%) and venues (40%). Another 23% said they "Mostly figured it out myself".

5.2 What skills do project members contribute most?

🧠
Key point

Topic research and project management are the most common skills (51% each); coding ranks only sixth (28%).

Read more

Skills project members contributed (more than one answer allowed): topic research and project management tie for first at 51% each, followed by marketing and outreach (45%), admin and logistics (40%) and writing (38%). Coding is sixth at 28%. The rest are building rapport and relationships (23%), UX/UI design (17%) and visual design (15%).

You don't have to code to take part in a project. As Chapter 7 shows, 35% of the 26 Curious who have not taken part listed "I'd need to know how to code; the bar is too high" as a reason for not joining. But among the 47 Builders, fewer than three in ten wrote code; more worked on researching the topic, managing progress, promoting results and writing. This echoes the pain point in Chapter 3, stalled progress. From this we can even infer that projects may need people who can manage more than people who can code.

5.3 More than half of the Builders spend five hours or less a month on their project

⏱️
Key point

More than half of the Builders spend 5 hours or less a month on their project, but 21% spend 21 hours or more, close to a part-time job.

Read more

Asked how much time they spent on the project in the past 30 days: 1 to 5 hours was the most common answer (32%), followed by 6 to 20 hours and "almost none", tied at 23% each; 21 hours or more came to 21%.

More than half of the Builders spend less than 5 hours a month on their project, which echoes the problem in Chapter 3, "Progress is hard to keep steady": when most members move a project forward in spare moments, it is no surprise that progress comes in fits and starts. At the other end, 21% spend 21 hours or more a month, close to a part-time job. The community has both occasional contributors and people who are deeply committed.

6.2 At which stage do projects struggle most to find resources?

🚧
Key point

Funders think projects "still at a very early stage, just an idea" have the hardest time finding resources. For projects that already have a prototype, the fewest funders see finding resources as hard.

Read more

Of the 27 funders, 52% think projects "still at a very early stage, just an idea" have the hardest time finding resources, followed by projects "already launched and needing long-term upkeep" (37%). Projects that "have a prototype and are moving toward real-world use" are seen most positively: only 11% think they struggle to find resources. This ties in with Section 6.1: funders care about a clearly explained problem, and at the idea stage, without concrete results to show, it is harder to convince them.

7.2 What is the main barrier for people who have not taken part in civic tech?

🚪
Key point

The top three barriers for newcomers are all about missing information and a way in, not ability. Worrying about not being able to code ranks only fourth.

Read more

Among the 26 Curious who have not taken part (up to 3 reasons each): the top three reasons are not knowing which projects or issues exist (62%, 16/26), having no one around to show the way and worrying about not fitting in or being any help (54%, 14/26), and not knowing how to join or where to start (50%, 13/26). The stereotype that civic tech requires tech skills does not hold up: "I'd need to know how to code; the bar is too high" comes fourth, at 35% (9/26).

7.3 Which events do people at different stages want?

🎓
Key point

The Curious most want "Intro to the database"; the Builders most want "Working with government". Different groups need different things.

Read more

The survey asked which events people would most like to sign up for (up to 2 choices). Across all respondents, the most popular was "Working with government" (57%), followed by "Civic tech in practice" (51%) and "Intro to the database" (49%). "Pitching with AI" was chosen least (33%).

Comparing the three groups shows different preferences. "Intro to the database" is the most popular among the 26 Curious, at 69% (18/26), but only 36% (17/47) among the 47 Builders, possibly because people already doing projects tend to know the existing projects and need an introduction less. "Working with government" is the opposite: it is the event the Builders most want (62%, 29/47), while demand among the 26 Curious is relatively low (46%, 12/26).

For community organizers, it helps to decide who an event is for before planning it: people who have not yet had contact with civic tech, people still standing at the door deciding whether to join, or people already working on projects?

IV. Recommendations

1. Two things to do first: help people find projects, and explain clearly how results can be put into real use

1.1 How to decide which topics to write about first

🎯
Key point

All five candidate article topics are "Attractive": having them is a plus, but not having them would not cause complaints.

We used Kano analysis (the Kano model), a method from product design that sorts features by how having or lacking them affects satisfaction, to see how respondents value the article topics g0v.tw Jothon is planning:

  • Attractive: a pleasant surprise if it is there, no complaints if it is not. A bonus.
  • Performance: the more you do, the more satisfied people are; without it, they are disappointed. The most reliable return on effort.
  • Must-be: taken for granted if it is there, a source of dissatisfaction if it is not. A basic requirement; leaving it out feels like something is missing.
  • Indifferent: people don't care either way. The lowest priority.
Read more

The results show that all five candidate article topics fall into "Attractive", with "How to get what you built into real use, including within government" closest to "Performance". These topics add value for respondents, but when they answered, none had reached the basic level of "people would complain if it were missing".

Looking at the three groups separately shows one difference: "How to get more people involved" and "How to keep partners" are "Indifferent" for the 26 Curious, who have heard of or seen civic tech but not got involved, yet "Attractive" for both other groups. This may reflect that people who have not engaged deeply with civic tech feel the pain of running a team and keeping it going less keenly.

1.2 Topics and tools: budgets and procurement, and crowdsourcing tools, are most wanted

📚
Key point

Among cross-cutting tools, the most wanted introduction is "Crowdsourcing and reporting tools" (61%); among issue areas, the most wanted is "government budgets, spending and procurement" (54%).

Read more

Besides article topics, the survey asked which issue areas respondents most want data lists for, and which cross-cutting tools they most want introduced (both questions allowed more than one answer):

Cross-cutting tools: crowdsourcing and reporting tools were chosen most (61%), followed by AI applications (54%) and data visualization tools (52%), then GIS and mapping tools (42%), opinion-gathering and online deliberation tools (33%), finding existing research (31%), and finally open collaborative knowledge bases (19%).

Issue areas: the data lists respondents most want to see first are government budgets, spending and procurement (54%), local development and regional revitalization (47%), disaster prevention and response (43%), and misinformation and scam prevention (42%), with environmental pollution (31%) and law and the legislative process (31%) tied after them.

Comparing these tools and issue areas across the three groups shows that their needs and interests differ slightly.

Chapter 7 shows that the biggest barrier for newcomers is not knowing which projects or issues they could join, not a lack of technical skill or ability. With these basics in place, the next question is: how can the database reach each newcomer at the right moment, just as they want to get started?

How could a better way of introducing newcomers be designed? Or does stepping through the door simply require newcomers to put in more effort themselves? These are questions worth continuing to explore.

1.4 How to support the ecosystem: early projects lack resources most, and networks are used more than money

🤝
Key point

Early projects lack resources most, personal networks are used more than money, and those with the most say are most likely to burn out: all three are worth noting for anyone who provides resources.

If you might provide resources, here are a few things from this survey worth knowing:

Early projects have the hardest time finding resources, and this comes from funders themselves. Chapter 6 shows that more than half of funders (14 of 27, 52%) think projects "still at a very early stage, just an idea" have the hardest time finding resources. If you are thinking about how to support this ecosystem, this is a place to start: the earlier the support, the scarcer it tends to be, and the more it may decide whether a good idea ever grows.

Support is not only money. Chapter 5 shows that personal networks are the resource Builders use most; more of them had used networks than funding. Introducing an expert to consult, connecting a team with the right government contact, or offering a space to meet are all forms of support that project teams have actually used.

Look out for the core members holding projects together. Chapter 3 found that the more say people have over a project's direction, the more likely they are to burn out: 6 of the 10 (60%) who started a project said core-member burnout clearly held back progress. Chapter 4 also found that the sense of belonging to the community fades the most over time. Taken together, these may be a reminder for anyone supporting a project over the long term: besides tracking results, it is worth checking whether the few people holding the team together can keep going.

V. Reflections

1. Alternative explanations for each conclusion

Each chapter offered another possible explanation alongside its main findings. This section brings these alternative explanations together, so readers can check them and judge which conclusions can be relied on and which are better treated as directions for further observation.

This report's conclusionAnother possible explanation
The 27 funders represent the views of funders (Chapter 6) 26 of the 27 are already part of the community. The survey was shared through g0v.tw channels, so the funders it reached were mostly insiders. These are insiders' views, not those of outside funders such as foundations, companies or governments.
Personal networks are used more than funding (Chapter 5) The question asked "which have you used", not "which are hardest to get". Networks may be used more simply because they are easier to come by; this does not mean funding is unimportant or not scarce.
The sense of belonging is the motive that fades most (Chapter 4) The survey only reached people still in the community. If belonging really fails to keep people, those people may have left long ago and never answered the survey, so the drop may actually be underestimated.
People aged 41 and over stay mainly because they "believe in the goal" (Chapter 4) This may be a generational difference, or it may be that those who stay have already been filtered: older people still in the community are more likely to be mission-driven to begin with.
What holds newcomers back is information, not technology (Chapter 7) These 26 people have already heard of or seen civic tech, but not taken part; they are not the general public who have never heard of it. For people with no contact at all, the barriers may lie elsewhere.
Six in ten live in Taipei or New Taipei (Chapter 1) This reflects a limit of the sample: the survey was shared through g0v.tw channels, and g0v.tw's in-person events have long been held in Taipei, so it cannot be taken as the community's real geographic spread.

Limitations to keep in mind

  • The survey was shared through g0v.tw and related community channels, so respondents lean toward people already interested in civic tech. The conclusions apply only to these respondents, not to the general public.
  • Results for questions answered by all 127 respondents are relatively stable. But project-level questions (stage, resources, difficulties, motives) were answered only by the 47 Builders; split further, each group has only a dozen or so people, so treat those results as directions worth checking again.
  • All cross-tabulations show correlation, not causation.
  • The live version of the difficulties question had no "not experienced" option, so average scores are systematically underestimated. The main measure is therefore always "3 or higher (3, 4 and 5 combined)".
