### [intro-02]

<!-- 正文開始 intro-02 -->
This report analyzes a survey sent out in 2026 by Jothon, a working group within [g0v.tw](https://g0v.tw/)[^1], Taiwan's grassroots civic tech community. With personal details removed, the raw responses are [open to everyone](https://docs.google.com/spreadsheets/d/1Z7am3yalgPbQLureQS0tWTofSKrLJRAI4473pFLErag/edit?usp=sharing) under CC BY 4.0, credited to the survey respondents ("臺灣公民科技推廣問卷填寫者").
<!-- 正文結束 intro-02 -->

### [intro-03]

<!-- 正文開始 intro-03 -->
The survey gathered 127 responses up to 20 July 2026. We wanted to see what Taiwan's civic tech community really looks like: who takes part, and what people at different levels of involvement need and struggle with. By finding out what draws people in and where they get stuck, we hope to help [g0v.tw](https://g0v.tw/) Jothon put its resources where they matter most and keep Taiwan's civic tech community thriving.
<!-- 正文結束 intro-03 -->

### [intro-04]

<!-- 正文開始 intro-04 -->
Put simply, civic tech means using technology and digital tools to make public life better. It can take many forms: building a small tool that makes it easier to take part in public affairs or use public services, or bringing people together to discuss policy and using open data to improve how government works. Open data here means data that governments or organizations publish for anyone to freely use. Examples include the "mask map", which helped people in Taiwan find face masks during COVID-19, and vTaiwan, where people with different views discuss policy online.
<!-- 正文結束 intro-04 -->

### [intro-05]

<!-- 正文開始 intro-05 -->
If you want to go straight to projects you could join, [Civic Tech Taiwan](https://civictech.tw/), a database run by [g0v.tw](https://g0v.tw/) Jothon, lists many projects and guides to getting involved (in Chinese). It is a good place to start.
<!-- 正文結束 intro-05 -->

### [summary-01]

<!-- 正文開始 summary-01 -->
II. Summary: four key findings
<!-- 正文結束 summary-01 -->

### [summary-02]

<!-- 正文開始 summary-02 -->
**1. Stalled projects and burned-out core members are the biggest pain for Builders**
<!-- 正文結束 summary-02 -->

### [summary-03]

<!-- 正文開始 summary-03 -->
At every [g0v.tw](https://g0v.tw/) bimonthly hackathon, you see plenty of projects looking for people to "jump in" and build with them. But for people who work on projects, the biggest headache is not a shortage of people. It is keeping progress steady, getting results to make a difference, and core members burning out. In this survey, 32% (15/47) of the 47 Builders had no active project when they answered. Progress that stops and starts, results that don't land, and a few people holding on for a long time: together, these three are the biggest obstacle. Among these respondents, **those with more say in a project tend to be more likely to burn out.** This is quite likely because more say usually means more effort and time, which makes people more tired.
<!-- 正文結束 summary-03 -->

### [summary-04]

<!-- 正文開始 summary-04 -->
**2. Believing in what a project wants to do, and making like-minded friends, make people more likely to stay**
<!-- 正文結束 summary-04 -->

### [summary-05]

<!-- 正文開始 summary-05 -->
The strongest pull, both for bringing people into civic tech projects and for keeping them, is believing in what the project aims to achieve. It is the most common reason people join, and the most lasting reason they stay. Another change worth noting: the motive "build up a portfolio and sharpen my skills" grew from 6 people at first to 9 now, a relative increase of 50%. This shows that some people keep taking part to build their skills.
<!-- 正文結束 summary-05 -->

### [summary-06]

<!-- 正文開始 summary-06 -->
**3. Not knowing what projects exist, and having no one to show the way, are the biggest barriers for newcomers**
<!-- 正文結束 summary-06 -->

### [summary-07]

<!-- 正文開始 summary-07 -->
Among the 26 Curious who have not taken part, the most common barriers were "don't know which projects or issues exist" and "no one around to show me the way, and I worry I won't fit in or be any help", followed by "don't know how to join or where to start". "I feel I'd need to know how to code; the bar is too high", which many people assume is the main obstacle, actually came only fourth. 65% of all respondents regularly see news from at least one official [g0v.tw](https://g0v.tw/) channel, but **seeing the news does not mean knowing what you can do, or knowing someone you can ask**.
<!-- 正文結束 summary-07 -->

### [summary-08]

<!-- 正文開始 summary-08 -->
**4. A clearly explained problem and a working prototype attract support most easily**
<!-- 正文結束 summary-08 -->

### [summary-09]

<!-- 正文開始 summary-09 -->
Among the 27 respondents who have put money into civic tech projects, what mattered most when deciding whether to support one was "the problem it tackles is important and clearly explained", and whether it "matches issues I care about". More than half of these funders also felt that projects "still at a very early stage, just an idea" have the hardest time getting resources. This makes sense: projects that have not yet turned an idea into a working prototype usually find it harder to win funders over.
<!-- 正文結束 summary-09 -->

1.1 Who they are and where they are

### [ch1-1-02]

### [ch1-1-03]

<!-- 正文開始 ch1-1-03 -->
The 127 responses fall roughly into three groups:
<!-- 正文結束 ch1-1-03 -->

### [ch1-1-04]

<!-- 正文開始 ch1-1-04 -->
**The Curious**: 26 people who have heard of or seen civic tech, but have not actually got involved.
<!-- 正文結束 ch1-1-04 -->

### [ch1-1-05]

<!-- 正文開始 ch1-1-05 -->
**The Users**: 54 people who know the community but have not worked on any project.
<!-- 正文結束 ch1-1-05 -->

### [ch1-1-06]

<!-- 正文開始 ch1-1-06 -->
**The Builders**: 47 people who have actually worked on at least one civic tech project. Later questions about "what got stuck" and "what keeps people going" were answered only by these 47, because they asked about specific project experience.
<!-- 正文結束 ch1-1-06 -->

### [ch1-1-07]

<!-- 正文開始 ch1-1-07 -->
**Most respondents are aged 31 to 50**: 35% are 31 to 40 and 29% are 41 to 50, while only 5.5% are 20 or under. By gender, 50% are men, 41% women, and 9% other or prefer not to say.
<!-- 正文結束 ch1-1-07 -->

### [ch1-1-08]

<!-- 正文開始 ch1-1-08 -->
**Respondents come from a wide range of work backgrounds**: freelancers and nonprofit workers are the largest groups, at 20% each, followed by schools or research institutions (16%), companies or startups (16%), students (13%) and the public sector (8%). Of the 55 who told us more about their organization, 27% work at nonprofits and associations, 25% at academic and research institutions, and 22% at companies and startups. People who care about civic tech come from many industries and organizations, not one field.
<!-- 正文結束 ch1-1-08 -->

### [ch1-1-09]

<!-- 正文開始 ch1-1-09 -->
But looking only at where people live understates how far their connections reach. The survey also asked "Besides where you live, which regions do you have close ties to?" 63 people answered: 34 named places in central, southern or eastern Taiwan (such as Taichung, Tainan, Kaohsiung and Hualien), and 14 mentioned places overseas. Even among the 79 who live in Taipei or New Taipei, 20 also have close ties to central, southern or eastern Taiwan, and 8 to places overseas. Most respondents live in the capital region, but their personal networks reach much further.
<!-- 正文結束 ch1-1-09 -->

### [ch2-1-01]

<!-- 正文開始 ch2-1-01 -->
2.1 What roles have people played in civic tech projects?
<!-- 正文結束 ch2-1-01 -->

### [ch2-1-02]

<!-- 正文開始 ch2-1-02 -->
Of the 101 who have had contact with civic tech, nearly nine in ten have been a "User". Only 33% have actually helped build or maintain a project.
<!-- 正文結束 ch2-1-02 -->

### [ch2-1-03]

<!-- 正文開始 ch2-1-03 -->
The survey asked which roles people have played in civic tech projects (more than one answer allowed). Among the 101 who have had contact with civic tech: 89% have used a tool or joined a discussion, 48% have helped put something into use or spread it, 33% have helped plan, build, design or maintain a project, 28% have started a project, and 17% have provided money or mobilized resources.
<!-- 正文結束 ch2-1-03 -->

### [ch2-1-04]

<!-- 正文開始 ch2-1-04 -->
46 people (46%) ticked only one role; just 10 (10%) ticked all five. **A large group sticks to one thing, while a small group does almost everything**, and the spread in between is uneven. The more deeply involved people are, the more roles they usually take on at once.
<!-- 正文結束 ch2-1-04 -->

### [ch2-1-05]

<!-- 正文開始 ch2-1-05 -->
The survey also asked a single-choice question: "Which statement best describes what you do in the civic tech community?" The five options run from the least to the most say over a project's direction: Follow, Report, Join, Co-design and Start (another 6 people chose "None of these fits me", which is left off this scale). Of the 101, 41% chose the lightest level, "Follow".
<!-- 正文結束 ch2-1-05 -->

### [ch2-1-06]

<!-- 正文開始 ch2-1-06 -->
Of the 46 who ticked only one role, 42 ticked the one with the lowest bar, "used a tool or joined a discussion", and 30 of them were at the "Follow" level of involvement. "Doing only one thing" largely reflects "being lightly involved".
<!-- 正文結束 ch2-1-06 -->

### [ch2-1-07]

<!-- 正文開始 ch2-1-07 -->
One more thing worth noting: **people who build projects have not necessarily used other people's civic tech**. 38 people ticked "helped plan, build, design or maintain" or "started a project" (counting those who ticked both only once), and 7 of them (18%) did not tick "used a tool or joined a discussion".
<!-- 正文結束 ch2-1-07 -->

### [ch2-2-01]

<!-- 正文開始 ch2-2-01 -->
2.2 What are these projects about?
<!-- 正文結束 ch2-2-01 -->

### [ch2-2-02]

<!-- 正文開始 ch2-2-02 -->
Among the 47 Builders, the most common project topic was environment and ecology (15%). The rest are spread across 10 areas such as education, disaster response and political oversight.
<!-- 正文結束 ch2-2-02 -->

### [ch2-2-03]

<!-- 正文開始 ch2-2-03 -->
Each of the 47 described in one sentence "what problem their most recent project aims to solve". After sorting these by hand into 11 categories, the most common topic was environment and ecology, with 7 people (15%). Second was "Other", with 6 people (13%), covering topics such as housing, health care, energy and digital identity. Next came education and learning, community-building and collaboration tools, and disaster prevention and public safety, with 5 people each (11%); then political oversight and open parliament, land use, cities and local communities, and public discussion and opinion tools, with 4 each (9%). The rest were transport and roads (3 people), public-private collaboration and civil servants (2), and fact-checking misinformation (2).
<!-- 正文結束 ch2-2-03 -->

### [ch2-2-04]

<!-- 正文開始 ch2-2-04 -->
Of these, 15 people's projects deal with topics that require working with the public sector. This fits with Chapter 7, where "Public-private collaboration" was the most wanted event.
<!-- 正文結束 ch2-2-04 -->

### [ch2-2-05]

<!-- 正文開始 ch2-2-05 -->

The two are kept separate, because someone who often starts projects in the community may not be the founder of the project they worked on most recently.

### [ch3-01]

<!-- 正文開始 ch3-01 -->
3. What wears Builders down most: stalled progress and burned-out core members
<!-- 正文結束 ch3-01 -->

### [ch3-1-01]

<!-- 正文開始 ch3-1-01 -->
3.1 What troubles people most when working on a project?
<!-- 正文結束 ch3-1-01 -->

### [ch3-1-02]

<!-- 正文開始 ch3-1-02 -->
Among the 47 Builders, the top three problems were keeping progress steady, getting results to make a difference, and a few people holding everything up. "Not finding partners", the one most people would think of first, came only fourth.
<!-- 正文結束 ch3-1-02 -->

### [ch3-1-03]

<!-- 正文開始 ch3-1-03 -->
Only the 47 Builders answered these questions. The survey listed 13 common difficulties and asked them to rate each one. We use two measures: a rating of 3 or higher (3, 4 and 5 combined) means the problem really held back progress; 4 or higher (4 and 5 combined) means it was bad enough to make them want to quit.
<!-- 正文結束 ch3-1-03 -->

### [ch3-1-04]

<!-- 正文開始 ch3-1-04 -->
**The top three problems were keeping progress steady, getting results to make a difference, and a few people holding everything up. "Can't find partners who want to work on it together", the one most people would think of first, came fourth.** These three may feed into each other: when momentum breaks, results are slow to appear; without results, it is harder to show the project is worth the effort; fewer people step in, and the load falls back on the few.
<!-- 正文結束 ch3-1-04 -->

### [ch3-2-01]

<!-- 正文開始 ch3-2-01 -->
3.2 The more say people have, the more likely they are to burn out
<!-- 正文結束 ch3-2-01 -->

### [ch3-2-02]

<!-- 正文開始 ch3-2-02 -->
Among the Builders there is a pattern: the more say someone has in a project, the more likely they are to feel burned out. Of the 10 who started their own project, 6 (60%) said core-member burnout clearly held back progress.
<!-- 正文結束 ch3-2-02 -->

|---|---|---|---|

<!-- 正文結束 ch3-2-03 -->

### [ch3-2-04]

<!-- 正文開始 ch3-2-04 -->
Among these respondents, those more deeply involved and with more say were more likely to face core-member burnout (quite likely their own), and more worried that the results would not make a difference.
<!-- 正文結束 ch3-2-04 -->

### [ch3-3-01]

<!-- 正文開始 ch3-3-01 -->
3.3 Decision-makers don't see what newcomers struggle with
<!-- 正文結束 ch3-3-01 -->

### [ch3-3-02]

<!-- 正文開始 ch3-3-02 -->
When planning how to bring newcomers in, asking only founders and decision-makers may not get you the answer: it is usually not a problem they face themselves.
<!-- 正文結束 ch3-3-02 -->

### [ch3-3-03]

<!-- 正文開始 ch3-3-03 -->
Now look at the item "Newcomers or people who can't code don't know how they can help". The people who feel it most are at the shallow end: 2 of 6 (33%) in the Report group, 1 of 5 (20%) in the Follow group, 1 of 8 (12%) who joined someone else's project, 0% (0/18) in the Co-design group, and 1 of 10 (10%) who started a project.
<!-- 正文結束 ch3-3-03 -->

### [ch3-3-04]

<!-- 正文開始 ch3-3-04 -->
People less involved were more likely to worry about how newcomers who can't code could help. As people's say in a project grows, this worry broadly tends to be lower. **So if you want to plan how to bring newcomers in, asking only founders and decision-makers may not get you the answer, because it is not a problem they face themselves.**
<!-- 正文結束 ch3-3-04 -->

### [ch4-1-02]

|---|---|---|---|
| Believe in what it is trying to achieve | 39 | 92% | 0% |

### [ch4-1-04]

<!-- 正文開始 ch4-1-04 -->
"Retention rate" means the same person chose the same motive both times. "Net change" looks at how the number of people rose or fell: those who chose a motive when they first joined, plus those who picked it up later, minus those who no longer give it as a reason. The two look at different things, so they rank motives differently. For example, "People around me were doing it, so I joined" had the lowest retention rate of the six, at 67%; but because the 4 people who dropped it matched the 4 who picked it up, its net change is still 0% and the total did not change.
<!-- 正文結束 ch4-1-04 -->

### [ch4-1-05]

<!-- 正文開始 ch4-1-05 -->
Believing in a project's goal is the most common reason people take part, and the reason that best sustains them over time.
<!-- 正文結束 ch4-1-05 -->

### [ch4-1-06]

<!-- 正文開始 ch4-1-06 -->
Identifying with the community had the biggest net drop of the six motives: 20 people chose it at first, and 17 do now. Of the original 20, 6 (30%) no longer give it as a reason, while 3 others picked it up later. This is worth looking into: did the people who stopped naming the community as a reason gradually lose their sense of belonging along the way?
<!-- 正文結束 ch4-1-06 -->

<!-- 正文開始 ch4-1-07 -->
Only two motives grew overall: "building a portfolio and sharpening skills" and "Meet new people and like-minded friends". The first grew more, and it is a goal-oriented motive.

1.4 How to support the ecosystem: early projects lack resources most, and networks are used more than money
<!-- 正文結束 rec-1-4-01 -->

### [rec-1-4-02]

<!-- 正文開始 rec-1-4-02 -->
Early projects lack resources most, personal networks are used more than money, and those with the most say are most likely to burn out: all three are worth noting for anyone who provides resources.
<!-- 正文結束 rec-1-4-02 -->

### [rec-1-4-03]

<!-- 正文開始 rec-1-4-03 -->
If you might provide resources, here are a few things from this survey worth knowing:
<!-- 正文結束 rec-1-4-03 -->

### [rec-1-4-04]

<!-- 正文開始 rec-1-4-04 -->
**Early projects have the hardest time finding resources, and this comes from funders themselves.** Chapter 6 shows that more than half of funders (14 of 27, 52%) think projects "still at a very early stage, just an idea" have the hardest time finding resources. If you are thinking about how to support this ecosystem, this is a place to start: the earlier the support, the scarcer it tends to be, and the more it may decide whether a good idea ever grows.

### [rec-1-4-05]

<!-- 正文開始 rec-1-4-05 -->
**Support is not only money.** Chapter 5 shows that personal networks are the resource Builders use most; more of them had used networks than funding. ### [rec-1-4-06]

<!-- 正文開始 rec-1-4-06 -->
**Look out for the core members holding projects together.** Chapter 3 found that the more say people have over a project's direction, the more likely they are to burn out: 6 of the 10 (60%) who started a project said core-member burnout clearly held back progress. Chapter 4 also found that the sense of belonging to the community fades the most over time. Taken together, these may be a reminder for anyone supporting a project over the long term: besides tracking results, it is worth checking whether the few people holding the team together can keep going.

### [limits-01]

Limitations to keep in mind
<!-- 正文結束 limits-01 -->

### [limits-02]

<!-- 正文開始 limits-02 -->
The survey was shared through g0v.tw and related community channels, so respondents lean toward people already interested in civic tech. The conclusions apply only to these respondents, not to the general public.
<!-- 正文結束 limits-02 -->

### [limits-03]

<!-- 正文開始 limits-03 -->
Results for questions answered by all 127 respondents are relatively stable. But **project-level questions** (stage, resources, difficulties, motives) were answered only by the 47 Builders; split further, each group has only a dozen or so people, so treat those results as directions worth checking again.
<!-- 正文結束 limits-03 -->

### [limits-04]

<!-- 正文開始 limits-04 -->
All cross-tabulations show correlation, not causation.
<!-- 正文結束 limits-04 -->

### [limits-05]
